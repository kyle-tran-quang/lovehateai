### What happens

Posters repeat in treatment but not in colour, so the sequence reads as a set of explorations around three ideas: a repeated or sliced profile, a colour overlay of dots or pixel blocks, and a face assembled from small type.

### Timing and feel
Each poster holds for about 0.3s (a frame at 0.25s plus a near-identical one at 0.05s, so 3 or 4 posters a second). The cuts are instant: no fade, wipe or slide between posters. The rhythm is steady and quick, like a slideshow set to a fast clock. The whole thing is 7.2s and ends on the 24th poster; whether it loops is not something the file shows. The two frames per poster differ only by noise, so the posters themselves are still.

### What it is made of
All posters share a portrait of the same man, a handwritten wordmark ("First of Kind", hand-lettered capitals) or a plain grotesque in the pixel-block versions, and a tiny mono-style type for season, episode and names. Treatments seen: a photo cut into narrowing vertical slices, duotones (cyan, magenta/violet), marbled ink textures, blocky pixel patterns (blue, grey-black, pale blue), dots in solid colours (about #ffd21e yellow, #f26a2e orange, #3b5bff blue, #d46bff violet, approximate), outline rectangles, and portraits drawn with tiny words. Paper-like grain is visible on the black ones. The marks look like finished layouts, so it is a set of static frames, not an animation.

### To recreate the look
Inferred from the footage, untested.
1. Pick one photo and make a dozen very different poster layouts from it. Keep the same small type block and wordmark in each, so the set still reads as one identity.
2. Export each layout at the same square size.
3. Put them in a sequence with hard cuts and about 0.3s per frame. No transitions.
4. Keep the sequence unsorted, so neighbouring posters differ in colour and texture. That contrast between neighbours is most of the effect.
